Complexity, Reality, and Rationality in Large Nonlinear Equations Solving

Rojas' proposed project combines ideas from four separate areas of computational science: numerical analysis, algorithmic complexity, algebraic geometry, and number theory. Rojas' advances in these areas has opened up new possibilities in computational algebraic geometry and will yield many new and faster algorithms, and many sharper quantitative estimates, for understanding the geometry of the solutions of systems of equations. (For example, Rojas discovered the first explicit arithmetic analogue of fewnomial theory, a result which continues to grow in importance.) Rojas enjoys interacting with students so he will also begin a series of expository lectures at a local high school, thus opening young students minds to the beauties and possibilities of mathematics.

Broader Impacts Solving systems of nonlinear equations is ubiquitous in many applications. Applications of the algorithms Rojas has derived, and will derive, cut across many areas of engineering and science: A brief list includes drug design, faster and more reliable algorithms for radar direction finding and geometric modelling, and more reliable and efficient approaches to control theory. Rojas also plans to take release time to visit schools in poorer areas of Texas to help disadvantaged students and aggressively recruit more bright young people into the computational sciences. At two of these schools (Texas A&M at Prairie View and Texas A&M at Kingsville), minority enrollment is quite high: about 85% African American at Prairie View, and about 65% at Kingsville. Rojas also plans to continue supervising software projects, so that the applied communities can benefit from the discoveries of this project.

Real Solving and Protein Structures: Interdisciplinary Training for Undergraduates in Biological and Mathematical Sciences

Rojas' proposed supplemental project will leverage his algorithmic work on systems of multivariate analytic equations over the real numbers with recent collaborative work with Professor Jerry Tsai (Biochemistry, Texas A&M) on determining the structure and function of proteins. The main goals can be highlighted as follows:

  • Bringing four undegraduate students up to speed on the most modern mathematical approaches to understanding proteins. The best students will participate in making predictions for the biannual Critical Assessment of Structure Prediction (CASP) and attend the corresponding CASP meeting.
  • A deeper exploration of the connections between protein folding, real analytic geometry, and computational algebra.
  • Developing algebraic and geometric software for analyzing protein structure and establishing the computational resources necessary to solve the large nonlinear systems of equations arising from protein structure problems.

We thus propose to work on an important concrete application (structural proteomics) for one of the main thrusts (efficiently solving large nonlinear systems of equations) of Rojas' original grant proposal.

The technical details are detailed in the separate Justification of Supplement. Briefly, the approach combines recent work of Rojas on the real Nullstellensatz and numerical conditioning for structured systems of equations, older physically motivated potential functions for scoring protein folds, and the use of massive data sets from protein databases. In particular, the author will work closely with Professor Jerry Tsai of the Texas A&M University Biochemistry department.

Rojas and Tsai will provide an active learning environment for the four specially chosen undergraduate students. This will also include summer Research Experiences for other interested undergraduates, in consultance with Professor Jay Walton, another biomathematical expert in the Texas A&M University Mathematics department.

The monetary aspects consist of stipends for four undergraduate students, travel money for the PI and the four undergraduates to attend an upcoming CASP meeting, 2 high-end PC's for computational work, and a color laser printer for day to day illustration and visualization work.

Broader Impacts: Understanding a protein reduces to two main problems: the prediction of its structure (i.e., how it folds) and its function (e.g., what activity it influences in a cell or organ). These two problems are of tremendous importance in biochemistry and medicine. For instance, many famous diseases arise from proteins folding improperly: sickle cell anemia, Alzheimer's disease, Creutzfeldt-Jacobi (a.k.a. mad cow) disease, hereditary emphysema, and cystic fibrosis. Indeed, since genetic engineering is now sufficiently advanced to produce proteins with a given amino acid sequence, it makes sense to invest in understanding the behavior of proteins before one makes the effort to build them. Solving the structure prediction problem would thus help us solve the function prediction problem, which in turn would result in potentially huge benefits in drug design and combatting diseases.

Robust Output Sensitive Algorithms for Subanalytic Geometry

Rojas' proposed project is a unification and synthesis of ideas from three separate areas of computational science: numerical analysis, algorithmic complexity, and algebraic geometry. Recent developments at the intersection of these subjects, some contributed by Rojas himself, have enabled dramatic simplifications and algorithmic speed-ups for many formerly intractable computational problems. Applications cut across many areas of engineering and science and a brief list includes: more realistic modelling of neural net learning, faster and more reliable algorithms for radar direction finding and geometric modelling, certifiably correct solving of systems of multivariate real analytic inequalities, and new faster algorithms for p-adic cell decomposition.

The primary focus of the project is to complete the nascent theory of output sensitive algorithms in real algebraic geometry. Output sensitive in this context means that the complexity of the underlying algorithm depends mainly on intrinsic geometric parameters, e.g., the number of connected components of the underlying solution set, as opposed to extrinsic parameters like the degrees of the input polynomials. Such algorithms are faster than the traditional methods of computational algebra by a factor exponential in the dimension, but have so far been discovered only in various isolated contexts. So a unified algorithmic approach would have broad impact. Furthermore, Rojas' approach takes numerical conditioning into account from the outset. This care for robustness, precision, and certification of approximate solutions is novel in its application to non-linear algebra. In particular, the fact that much of the underlying theory applies to the more general context of real (and even p-adic) sub-analytic functions will be heavily exploited.

A secondary focus of the project is extending computational real algebraic geometry to the context of certain metrically complete fields, including the p-adic rationals. Based on a recent theoretical advance of Rojas, and earlier seminal work of Denef and van den Dries, it is now known that there is an arithmetic theory of fewnomials (valid over any finite algebraic extension of Q or Q_p), parallel to Khovanski's theory of fewnomials over R. The latter theory is a central ingredient in the algorithmic speed-ups mentioned earlier but, aside from the case of one polynomial in one variable, its counter-part over Q or Q_p has yet to be explored. Efficient multivariate polynomial system solving over the p-adics would yield a positive impact on numerous hard Diophantine and geometric problems. For example, estimating the average loss of precision for solving a random polynomial system (with integer coefficients) depends on estimating the Fubini-Study height of an algebraic variety, and the latter problem can be reduced to a p-adic integral of Igusa type. Taking another example, Kontsevich's technique of motivic integration (closely related to Igusa zeta functions) has yielded important new invariants for Calabi-Yau manifolds. Both of these examples can be approached via the technique of p-adic cell decomposition, which has p-adic polynomial system solving at its heart. These explorations would also greatly help our understanding of equation solving over finite fields, which is central in cryptology.

Degeneracy Detection for Curved Solids

We propose to study the use of toric resultants to detect degenerate situations in solid modeling applications. Degeneracies are a major source of robustness problems in many geometric algorithms. Geometric algorithms may assume that data is given in general position, although this assumption often fails in practice. Enumerating all possible degeneracies, detecting when these occur, and resolving the situations are all difficult tasks even for polyhedral models. Curved solides make these tasks even more difficult.

Resultants are a useful technique for solving several problems in computer algebra. Resultants have been successfully used to address problems in solid modeling, mainly through assistance in finding roots of algebraic equations (arising from surface and their intersections). Resultants can also be used to detect geometric degeneracies when the potential degenerate situation can be phrased as a simultaneous solution of several equations (e.g., three bivariate equations sharing a common root is a degeneracy.) The toric resultant, in particular, takes advantage of the underlying monomial term structure of the input polynomials to achieve a formulation that is significantly simpler than a more general resultant.

We propose to use the toric resultant to address problems of degeneracy detection in solid models. We focs specifically on the boundary evaluation problem. As a fundamental part of most solid modeling systems, boundary evaluation (i.e., find the boundary of Boolean combinations of curved solids) is an area of great interest. Furthermore, the operations involved in boundary evaluation, such as surface intersections and accurate representations of trimmed, curved patches, are fundamental aspects for many other areas of computer aided design. Thus, although the immediate focus will be on boundary evaluation, the results of this work should have a broader impact on CAD/CAM.

Because the detection of degenerate situations is highly prone to numerical error, and because resultant methods generally assume accurate input, we will focus our work on an exact computation model. We will use ESOLID, a system for exact boundary evaluation on low-degree curved solids, as a testbed for all new developments. Because common resultant methods may be slow, we will make a special effort to maximize the efficiency of the degeneracy detection algorithms. This will include not only optimizaing the computation algorithm itself, but also identifying ways that the detection routine can be avoided when it is ``clear'' that there is no degeneracy present. In the end, we hope to develop a set of efficient, practical routines, based on the toric resultant computation, that can be used to identify degeneracies in real-world boundary evaluation examples.

A major goal of this work will be to build a collaboration that will be beneficial for future research endeavors. By addressing this initial work, we believe that we will be well-positioned to jointly address other problems, including the resolution of degenarices in solid models, general algebraic number computation in geometric applications, and more accurate operations in computer aided design. The two graduate students supported by this proposal, through the necessary cross-training in both mathematics and computer science, will also be well-positioned for future work in this area. In addition, the PI's will work on the development of a joint course to be offered within the Computer Science and Mathematics departments that will focus on practical application of computer algebra techniques, preparing an even larger student base for continued work.

Numerical Stability and Statistical Algebraic Geometry

Even in 2004, many modern software packages for solving systems of polynomial equations still suffer from poor speed vs. accuracy trade-offs. This is due to a lack of a complete understanding of the degeneracies which can occur in various algorithms. It is worth noting that numerous applied problems ultimately reduce to solving a system of polynomial equations, hence the fundamental importance of polynomial system solving.

A modern approach, initiated by Edelman, Shub, Smale, and many others, is to view one's system of equations, F, as a point in some high-dimensional space. One then observes that the complexity of numerically solving F depends critically on how far F is from the ``ill-posed'' systems. The ill-posed systems usually form a hypersurface in one's space of problems, and one can then apply the tools of algebraic geometry and symplectic geometry.

For instance, one can estimate the probability that one's polynomial system is ill-posed or, equivalently, that it has a high condition number. (Interestingly, Alan Turing, the grandfather of algorithmic complexity, was one of the first researchers to define the condition number. So the connections between numerical analysis and algorithmic complexity go back all the way to the early 20th century.) This was first done for linear systems in the 1940's, and first done for non-linear systems by Shub and Smale around the mid-1990's.

More recently, sparsity (or the structure of one's equations) has proven to be a powerful tool for reducing the complexity of large systems of equations. So one must work harder, using toric variety theory sometimes, to derive results that take sparsity into account. This was done for the first time in the following paper:

  • Gregorio Malajovich and J. Maurice Rojas, ``High Probability Analysis of the Condition Number of Sparse Polynomial Systems,'' Theoretical Computer Science, (29 pages), to appear

    One can then naturally raise many quantitative questions on the distribution of the roots of random polynomial systems, and some of these questions are addressed in the above paper.

    An even harder question is the behavior of the real roots. This was addressed, for sparse systems, in the following paper:

  • J. Maurice Rojas, ``On the Average Number of Real Roots of Certain Sparse Polynomial Systems,'' Lectures in Applied Mathematics Series (edited by Jim Renegar, Mike Shub, and Steve Smale), vol. 32, American Mathematical Society, 1996, pp. 689-699.

    More recently, Rojas has been seeking even farther reaching results for random algebraic sets, and has been invited to Johns Hopkins University to work on some of these questions.

    Note: I first heard the term ``statistical algebraic geometry'' from my friend Steve Zelditch, of Johns Hopkins University.

    Computational Algebraic Geometry, e.g., Solving Systems of Polynomial Equations Over the Real and Complex Numbers

    I view computational algebraic geometry in the broadest possible sense: the interplay between algorithms and equations, considering solutions over any underlying ring. In particular, the computational nature of equation solving changes tremendously as one considers complex solutions, real solutions, rational solutions, and integer solutions, and I explore many of these questions.

    Algebraically Closed Fields

    Let us first consider a system of polynomial equations, F, over the complex numbers. One can then ask some basic questions about its set of complex solutions Z:

  • 1. What is the (complex) dimension of Z?
  • 2. If Z is finite, what is its cardinality?
  • 3. If Z is finite, can one approximate the coordinates of the points in Z in a fast and certifiably accurate way?
  • 4. What is the algorithmic complexity of all the above questions?

    It still surprises me that, after centuries of algebraic geometry and a tremendous revolution in the subject during the 20th century, (3) and (4) still lack definite answers.

    Some of my papers revolve around the above questions and give fast algorithms and complexity lower bounds. For instance, improving recent work of Koiran, I was able to find an algorithm which gives the best current complexity bound for Problem (1). (Koiran does so under the assumption of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis while I just showed that one can rely on an even more plausible hypothesis, which is likely provable with current technology.) This is covered in the following paper:

  • J. Maurice Rojas, ``Dedekind Zeta Functions and the Complexity of Hilbert's Nullstellensatz,'' paper corresponding to semi-plenary talk at FoCM 2002 (August 5-14, 2002, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis), submitted for publication.

    As for Problems (2) and (3), a beautiful discovery made close to three decades ago has resulted in some deep connections between convex geometry and polynomial systems. This began with work of A. Kouchnirenko, David N. Bernstein, and A. Khovanskii relating the ``average'' number of complex roots of a sparse polynomial system with, of all things, mixed volumes of polytopes. Part of my recent work deals with refining and generalizing this combinatorial approach to polynomial systems, and making it algorithmic and practical. For example, in the following papers:

  • Gregorio Malajovich and J. Maurice Rojas, ``High Probability Analysis of the Condition Number of Sparse Polynomial Systems,'' Theoretical Computer Science, to appear
  • John Junkins, Daniele Mortari, and J. Maurice Rojas, ``Attitude and Position Estimation from Vector Observations (extended abstract),'' Proceedings of the AAS/AIAA Space Flight Mechanics Meeting (February 8-12, 2004, Maui, Hawaii), to appear.
    ...we describe a modern approach to polynomial system solving based on toric/symplectic geometry, and apply it to a fundamental problem in space flight. An expository article I wrote on the application of toric geometry to polynomial system solving is the following paper:
  • J. Maurice Rojas, ``Why Polyhedra Matter in Non-Linear Equation Solving,'' paper corresponding to an invited talk delivered at a conference on Algebraic Geometry and Geometric Modelling (Vilnius, Lithuania, July 29-August 2, 2002), Contemporary Mathematics, AMS Press, vol. 334, pp. 293-320, 2003.
    ...and a paper containing explicit complexity bounds for solving arbitrary polynomial systems (some of them still the sharpest asymptotically), is the following:
  • J. Maurice Rojas, ``Solving Degenerate Sparse Polynomial Systems Faster,'' Journal of Symbolic Computation, vol. 28 (special issue on elimination theory), no. 1/2, July and August 1999, pp. 155-186.

    Real Closed Fields

    Consider now a system of polynomial equations F defined over the real numbers. One can then revisit the questions we raised above:
  • 1'. What is the (real) dimension of Z?
  • 2'. If Z is finite, what is its cardinality?
  • 3'. If Z is finite, can one approximate the coordinates of the points in Z in a fast and certifiably accurate way?
  • 4'. What is the algorithmic complexity of all the above questions?

    Curiously, even Problem (2') lacks a satisfactory answer: While it is now known, thanks to seminal work of Askold Khovanski, that the number real roots of F depends only weakly on the degree of the underlying equations, optimal bounds are an open question of the utmost interest. In particular, Khovanski's famous Theorem on Real Fewnomials (from the early 1980's) implies that the number of real roots can be bounded from above by a quantity depending only on the number of variables and monomial terms.

    However, the Theorem on Real Fewnomials is far from optimal: For the special case where F has 2 unknowns, and 2 equations with 3 monomial terms each, the Theorem on Real Fewnomials implies that F has no more than 284832 roots in the positive quadrant. But the correct sharp upper bound is 5. This, and much more, was first observed in the following paper:

  • Tien-Yien LI, J. Maurice Rojas, and Xiaoshen WANG, Counting Real Connected Components of Trinomial Curve Intersections and m-nomial Hypersurfaces, Discrete and Computational Geometry, 30 (2003), no. 3, pp. 379-414. There is thus reason to believe that one should be able to solve polynomial systems over the real numbers much faster than the traditional algorithms over the complex numbers. Further evidence appears in the following paper:
  • J. Maurice Rojas and Yinyu Ye, ``On Solving Fewnomials Over an Interval in Fewnomial Time,'' Journal of Complexity, to appear. I continue to work on these questions, which have deep impact on equation solving in practice.

    Computational Arithmetic Geometry, e.g., Solving Systems of Polynomial Equations Over the Rationals and Integers

    I've always loved the algorithmic side of number theory and have begun to look at certain problems which are on the verge of intractability.

    In particular, I've explored the complexity of certain Diophantine equations, and have found an unusual result on the computability of height bounds for integral points on curves. In a recent paper:

  • ``Uncomputably Large Integral Points on Algebraic Plane Curves?,'' submitted for publication
    ...I show that the decidability of a class of Diophantine problems in four variables implies that there are uncomputably large integral points on certain algebraic plane curves.

    As for rational points, it is interesting to note that the corresponding analogue of Hilbert's Tenth Problem is still open. For instance, we don't even have an algorithm for deciding whether an arbitrary input elliptic curve has a rational point. (That would be the special case of 1 polynomial, 2 variables, degree 3.)

    So it is natural to focus on invariants that could simplify the problem. For instance, what if one restricts to equations whose underlying set of complex zeroes is finite? Suprisingly, this still leads to subtle questions. For, while one can use results of the 1980's to get a complexity upper bound of PSPACE to find all the rational points, can one decide their mere existence faster? The first evidence that the answer is yes appears in the following paper:

  • J. Maurice Rojas, Computational Arithmetic Geometry I: Sentences Nearly in the Polynomial Hierarchy, invited paper (journal version of an earlier extended abstract that appeared in the Proceedings of the 31st Annual ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing (STOC '99, May 1-4, 1999, Atlanta, Georgia)), Journal of Computer and Systems Science, vol. 62 (2001), no. 2, march, pp. 216-235.

    On the more geometric end of arithmetic geometry, there is the problem of semi-stable reduction, which is a deepening of resolution of singularities. A special case of the semi-stable reduction problem can actually be reduced to questions on certain polyhedral subdivisions. This is covered in:

  • Dan Abramovich and J. Maurice Rojas, ``Extending Triangulations and Semistable Reduction,'' Proceedings of FoCM 2000, special meeting in honor of Steve Smale's 70th birthday (July 2000, City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong), World Scientific (2002), pp. 1-13.

    More recently, I am working on a deep connection between algebraic sets over finite fields and algebraic sets over the complex numbers. In particular, extending earlier work of Koiran, I gave a new number-theoretic algorithm for the old Hilbert's Nullstellensatz problem:

  • J. Maurice Rojas, ``Dedekind Zeta Functions and the Complexity of Hilbert's Nullstellensatz,'' paper corresponding to semi-plenary talk at FoCM 2002 (August 5-14, 2002, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis), Journal of Complexity, submitted for publication.
    The underlying algorithm is the fastest current method for deciding whether an input system of multivariate equations has a complex root, but relies on a (so far) unproven number-theoretic hypothesis called RIPIT. Nevertheless, RIPIT is significantly weaker than the famous Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (which most experts believe), so one of my current interests is exploring analytic number theory estimates in the hopes of proving RIPIT. The end goal is to prove (unconditionally) the following characterization of the P=NP problem: Let DIM denote the problem of deciding where an input system of equations has a complex zero set of dimension greater than some input integer d. Then DIM in P implies that P differs from NP.

    It is worth noting that the above implication is new, and currently known only under the assumption of RIPIT (see my paper above).
