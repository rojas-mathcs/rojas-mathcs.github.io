1+1=0 and Secure Information

Most of us would be suprised to learn that what looks like a simple math error, 1+1=0, forms the foundation of modern secure communications. However, to a cryptologist --- someone who studies code-making and code-breaking --- 1+1=0 is merely an accepted axiom for a new type of arithmetic which lends itself nicely to digital technology. We've all heard before that digital technology converts physical information (be it music, finger prints, movies, or bank statements) into streams of 0's and 1's. Storing these streams of digits onto disk, tape, or whatever media one uses, and then protecting that data from eavesdroppers, requires some clever packaging. Enter clever arithmetic with streams of 0's and 1's and the algebraic structures needed to implement it efficiently enough to run at, say, cell phone frequency (billions of times a second). The true domain of this branch of computational science lies at the intersection of number theory and algorithmic complexity, two areas that are now quite strong in the A&M mathematics department.

Of course, all this means little to someone who wants to buy something quickly on the web without their credit card number being stolen. And indeed, such practical concerns do ultimately shape the mathematical techniques one uses in building cryptosystems. For instance, common sense tells us that secure communications must be simple to use but (hopefully) incredibly hard to break without the keys to the code, i.e., a ``one-way'' function. Not coincidentally, there is now an active theory of one-way functions. Furthermore, researchers have discovered that one can create codes that are as hard to break as solving nearly intractable math problems. Put another way, this means that good cryptography forces your enemies to waste so much effort (hardware, money, and talent) that it would be in their best interests to leave your data alone. The focus then is on which problems are provably hard to solve and building cryptosystems as hard to crack as these hard problems are to solve. This type of research requires a broad point of view and keen insight to tie together seemingly disparate strands of computational science. One of A&M's recent tenure-track mathematics hires, J. Maurice Rojas (a former summer consultant to the Center for Communications Research) has, for example, given a precise characterization of the computational complexity of several formerly open number-theoretic problems. Full professors Bob Blakley and Itzak Borosh have deeply analyzed many modern cryptosystems based on other hard number-theoretic problems. On a historical note, it is interesting to note that Professor Blakley was the first to propose a method (and implementation) for secret sharing: In simple terms, he invented a scheme where a password could be broken into pieces and distributed to n allies in such a way that no smaller set of insiders could collude and recover the password faster than an outsider.

So how good is cryptography nowadays and can we really trust it? The true answer is controversial: We now have codes provably good enough that only a well-trained cryptological mathematician with millions (or billions) of dollars worth of computing power can have a chance of hacking through them in under a few months. However, this assumes (1) the code is used properly and that (2) there is no one smart enough to launch an even more efficient attack --- one new even to the top world-experts -- on one's code.

The first assumption is perhaps the most dangerous: there are now famous (recently declassified) stories of how the famous German ENIGMA code from World War II was broken. In addition to some undeniably brilliant work by allied cryptanalysts, there were some equally undeniably absurd mistakes made by the German military's use of ENIGMA which sped the allies' decoding. For instance, one German communications officer would repeatedly use his first name (Klaus) to key in the initial settings of his ENIGMA machine. So instead of searching over a space of billions of combinations, the allies could focus on just a few dozen common German names and break ENIGMA even faster.

The second assumption relies on good science and infrastructure: It is only through cutting edge mathematics that one can be sure that one's cryptosystem is not too easy to break. Most governments and large corporations already know this. Take for example the fact that the National Security Agency is the largest employer of mathematicians in the US. So it is only by raising the bar that one has any hope of building a cryptosystem that can withstand the attacks of a determined adversary.

The bar has already been raised very high: the most efficient techniques for breaking certain cryptosystems are now based on arithmetic geometry --- a deep and significant area of mathematics. Since the Fields Medals (the mathematical equivalent of the Nobel Prize) started being awarded in 1936, work in arithmetic geometry has been rewarded with a medal in all but four years (1936, 1962, 1990, and 1994). Paula B. Cohen, one of A&M's new full professors in mathematics who does work related to this field, is a collaborator with two of these Fields Medalists.

The continued growth of A&M's mathematics department, strategically focussed on vital areas of computational science, has succeeded in keeping A&M on the forefront of modern cryptological challenges. Expertise in cryptology is crucial to understanding and pushing back the limits of modern information technology. Homeland security is possible only when the theft of private information is infeasible, hence the need for sophisticated tools such as cryptology.
