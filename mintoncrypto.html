Slightly Edited Draft of Minton's Article...

Strengthening the defense of potential terrorist targets such as computer networks that control everything from train schedules to nuclear power plants, security experts are building tougher codes to protect electronic data. To hide private data (such as messages, archives, or passwords), code-builders wrap them in math problems so thorny that no one is likely to solve them.

Devising such problems is a problem in itself.

``You have to be clever. You have to use some really hard math,'' says J. Maurice Rojas, an assistant professor of mathematics at Texas A&M University. Rojas knows a sticky problem when he sees one: His expertise includes complexity theory, which is the art of classifying problems by how difficult they are to solve. To build a tight code, he says, requires a problem vexing enough to discourage the most determined saboteur - but not so tangled that a hardware chip can't at least send the code at a reasonable frequency. For instance, any slower than a billion times a second and your code will be unusable on a cell phone. So the final construction must also be tempered by mathematical elegance and good engineering.

Code-builders find their stumpers in fields of number theory and algorithmic complexity, branches of mathematics in which Texas A&M is increasingly strong.

The Department of Mathematics has recruited recent hires, such as Rojas, to complement longtime faculty such as George Robert Blakley. An internationally recognized expert on encryption algorithms and the theory of codes, Blakley is known for inventing a scheme in which a password may be broken up into pieces and distributed to a group of insiders so that no smaller group can recover the password. (So backing up passwords by distributing them across a large organization is a possibility.) His frequent collaborator on campus, Professor Itzak Borosh, also has deeply analyzed encryption systems and the number theory behind them. Meanwhile, Professor Paula Cohen, whose work has lead to collaborations with two Fields Medalists (math's equivalent of Nobel Laureates), explores the relation between number theory and physics. It is hoped that number theory will ultimately shed a new light on the mathematical logic of the universe.

Codes, and the math that makes them work, take on a new importance in the aftermath of Sept. 11, 2001. Now worried not only about the usual hackers but the likes of al Queda, the Bush administration is making cyber-security a linchpin of Homeland Security. International terrorists notwithstanding, more than 73,000 incidents [of what?] were reported through the nine month period ending Sept. 30, 2002, a third more than in all of 2001, according to the CERT Coordination Center, a clearinghouse for Internet security problems.

Facing this growing threat, experts search for ever harder math problems to make codes that will be even harder to crack, says Rojas. The research is demanding, requiring thinkers keen enough to pull together disparate strands of computational sciences. Although the action is rarely seen publicly (the movie ``A Beautiful Mind'' gave a rare glimpse), contriving mind-bending puzzles is nothing new in the world of higher math, whose great thinkers have made an academic sport of posing enigmas, conundrums, and hypotheses as challenges for all comers. Some have stood for centuries without solution. The Riemann Hypothesis, for instance, bubbled up from the mystery of how to quantify the seemingly irregular distribution of prime numbers (numbers divisible only by 1 and themselves) and remains without a proof after 143 years. The lack of a simple way to factor a number into primes underlies many important encryption schemes.

Even so, breaking encryption systems based on hard math problems is still a mathematical possibility. ``It is only by raising the bar that one has any hope of withstanding a determined adversary,'' Rojas says. The need to keep pushing the limits of science is what makes the fundamental research at Texas A&M crucial, he says. Some codes may look foolproof already. ``But you never know,'' he says. ``There might be a 17-year-old genius out there who can break it.''
